Australia has a fence so long it could stretch from New York to Los Angeles and keep going. It runs 5,600 kilometers—longer than the U.S.-Mexico border. And it wasn't built to stop people. It was built to stop dingoes. That fence marks the sharpest divide on Earth: the green, crowded coast on one side; the empty red desert on the other. Why is this line so brutal? Because of a mountain range called the Great Dividing Range. [curious, intimate] Look at a satellite map of Australia from space. You see a narrow strip of green along the southeast coast—then nothing but ochre for thousands of kilometers. That strip holds nearly all of Australia's 27 million people. The rest of the continent? Almost nobody. Here's the mechanism. The Pacific Ocean sends moisture-laden winds westward. They hit the Great Dividing Range, rise, cool, and drop their rain onto the coastal slopes. Sydney gets over a meter of rain per year. Then the air crosses the mountain crest and plunges down the far side, compressing and warming. [with growing energy] That hot, dry air sucks every drop of available moisture out of the soil. The rain shadow effect—a mountain-sized wall that turns half a continent into desert. [PAUSE] Travel just a few hundred kilometers inland, and rainfall collapses to under 250 millimeters a year. Too dry for crops. Too dry for towns. Too dry for people. That's why the eastern coast is a cramped ribbon of cities—and why the interior is the emptiest place on any inhabited continent. [serious] And that's where the Dingo Fence comes in. For thousands of kilometers, it runs along the edge of this desolation, protecting sheep and cattle from Australia's native wild dog. But really, it's a symbol: beyond this fence, the rain shadow has already won. Australia's emptiness isn't due to bad luck or policy. It's due to a simple, relentless fact: a mountain range steals the rain from an entire nation. [PAUSE] The fence doesn't keep people out. The rain shadow does.
